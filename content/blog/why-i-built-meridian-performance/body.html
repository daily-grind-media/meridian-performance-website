I didn't grow up as the person everyone expected to become a personal trainer.

I was the chubby kid growing up. For a long time, I wasn't happy with how I looked or how I felt about myself. By my senior year of high school, I had finally reached a point where I wanted to do something about it.

I remember walking into a supplement store and basically telling them that I was tired of being fat and out of shape. They helped me put together a basic meal plan and workout plan, and I started training.

That decision changed a lot more than my body.

As I started making progress, I became obsessed with understanding why it was working. I started learning about training, nutrition, recovery, habits and personal development. The more I learned, the more I realized how connected all of it was.

Getting stronger taught me discipline. Changing the way I ate taught me how much small decisions compound over time. Training consistently taught me that progress doesn't happen because you feel motivated every day.

It happens because you keep showing up.

From Engineering to Coaching

I eventually went to college for mechanical engineering.

Engineering made sense to me because I like understanding how things work. You look at a system, understand the inputs, identify what's preventing it from performing the way it should, and make adjustments.

But as I got further into engineering, I kept coming back to the same question.

What kind of impact do I actually want to have?

I realized that helping people change their lives meant more to me than building a traditional engineering career.

That didn't mean walking away from engineering meant walking away from the way I think. If anything, it became part of the way I coach.

I don't believe good personal training is just showing someone exercises for an hour.

You need to understand the person in front of you.

What are they trying to accomplish? What have they already tried? How are they training? How are they eating? How are they sleeping? What does their schedule actually look like? Where are they struggling to stay consistent?

Then you build around the answers.

Why Meridian

The name Meridian comes from astronomy.

A meridian represents the highest point the sun reaches in the sky, the peak of its arc.

That's what I want Meridian Performance to represent.

I'm not interested in helping someone get in shape for a few weeks and then go back to where they started. I want to help people work toward the highest version of themselves.

Obviously that includes the physical side.

You might want to lose body fat, build muscle, get stronger or simply feel more confident when you look in the mirror.

But I've experienced firsthand that changing yourself physically can affect everything else.

You start keeping promises to yourself.

You develop discipline.

You build confidence.

You handle difficult situations differently because you've proven to yourself that you're capable of doing hard things.

That's the part of coaching I'm most passionate about.

Most People Don't Need More Noise

There is an unbelievable amount of fitness information available now.

The problem for most people isn't a complete lack of information.

It's knowing what actually matters for them.

One person tells you to eliminate carbs. Someone else tells you to train six days a week. Another person tells you that one specific exercise is the secret to building muscle.

Meanwhile, you're trying to balance work, relationships, stress, sleep, meals, training and everything else happening in your life.

It's easy to get overwhelmed.

A big part of my job as a coach is removing that noise.

What do we actually need to focus on right now?

Maybe your training needs more structure. Maybe your nutrition is inconsistent. Maybe you're doing a lot of work in the gym but aren't progressing anything. Maybe your biggest issue is simply staying accountable long enough to see what a good plan can actually do.

We figure that out first.

Then we build from there.

Consistency Beats Perfection

One of the biggest mistakes I see people make is treating one bad day like they've ruined everything.

They miss a workout.

They have a bad weekend with food.

Work gets crazy for a week.

Suddenly they feel like they're back at zero.

You're not.

Fitness is a long game.

I've had to learn that in my own life, and it's something I try to teach every person I work with.

You don't need to execute perfectly every day. You need a system that allows you to keep moving forward even when life isn't perfect.

That means building training around your actual schedule.

It means developing nutrition habits you can realistically maintain.

It means measuring progress instead of relying entirely on how you feel that day.

And it means having someone hold you accountable when your own motivation isn't enough.

The Puzzle of Potential

I think about personal development as a puzzle.

Every good decision puts another piece into place.

Every workout you complete.

Every meal you improve.

Every time you choose discipline over convenience.

Every time you recover from a setback instead of quitting.

One decision isn't going to completely transform your life.

But enough of those decisions, repeated consistently, can change who you become.

That's what I want Meridian Performance to help people do.

My goal isn't to create clients who need me standing next to them forever. I want people to understand their training, understand their nutrition, become more confident in the gym and develop standards they can carry with them for the rest of their lives.
